# Q&A exemplars from the IIT corpus

Passages from the IIT wiki FAQs (Grasso, Hendren, Juel & Tononi, IIT Wiki, 2024) and
from Tononi & Boly 2025, quoted verbatim for study. Each passage has a note on the move
it makes. Read them to hear the pace and the argumentative line; then write your own
sentences. Do not paste or closely paraphrase them into an answer: reuse the *shape*
(an opening that fixes a term, a conceivability test, a verdict followed by a test),
never the content words.

## 1. Fixing a term before answering

*FAQ: What is meant by the term axiom in IIT?*

> The term *axiom* has different meanings. In regular speech, it refers to statements
> that are taken to be true because they are self-evident or obvious, and thus accepted
> without question. In modern logic and mathematics, it usually refers to an assumption
> that is true by stipulation—that is, a premise that is assumed to be true within a
> given formal system.
>
> In all these uses, an axiom is a starting assertion that requires no further
> justification. IIT shares this basic meaning of *axiom*, but it grounds the truth of
> its axioms not by stipulation but in experience itself—in phenomenal existence (see
> 0th axiom). Phenomenal existence is *immediate*, and the five axioms proposed by IIT
> aim to capture its essential properties—those that are *irrefutably true* of every
> conceivable experience. Let's examine these key notions in turn.

**Moves.** Opens by separating the ordinary senses of the word, then locates IIT's
sense against them ("IIT shares this basic meaning…, but…"). Em dashes are appositive
definitions ("—that is, a premise…", "—in phenomenal existence"). Italics mark the
terms being introduced. The last sentence announces structure only because the answer
really does take the notions one by one.

## 2. Restating the axiom, then answering the objection with cases

*FAQ: Can't I have a vague or generic experience, which isn't "specific"?*

> The information axiom states that every experience is *specific*. Some may try to
> argue that some experiences are "generic," but this is not the case.
>
> Take visual experience. You have been moving your eyes on this page. At each moment,
> you are having an experience that is specific: you are experiencing "this" image, even
> if you cannot introspect (let alone report) precisely how each image differs from the
> others. You might shift your eyes to a completely white wall and relax your focus to
> experience it as a "ganzfeld," with no sense of depth, movement, texture, shape, and so
> on—what could be more "vague" than that? But this too is the specific experience of
> hazy whiteness.
>
> […]
>
> That you are unable to specifically describe your experience does not mean that your
> experience is not specific. […] Experience is "what it is like to be" you, and the
> "what" is always specific—always a particular way.

**Moves.** First sentence restates the axiom at issue; second gives the verdict flatly.
Then a sequence of concrete cases addressed to "you", each chosen to be the hardest case
for the axiom (the ganzfeld), each resolved in one short sentence ("But this too is…").
The close separates the real point (describability) from the confusion and lands on a
substantive sentence, not a slogan.

## 3. The conceivability test

*FAQ: Why aren't the experiences of space and time considered axioms in IIT?*

> Try to imagine an experience without space or time. Even if this feels extremely
> difficult to do, such an experience is not "irrefutably true of every conceivable
> experience." For example, if we try to refute the axiom of integration by conceiving
> of an "unintegrated experience," we end up with two experiences, each of which is
> integrated; hence we demonstrate that integration is irrefutable because we end up
> with an absurdity or a contradiction. The same cannot be done for space or time. If we
> try, for example, to conceive of an experience without space, we don't inadvertently
> end up with an experience that is necessarily spatial. For example, we might conceive
> of a solely olfactory experience—say, of pure lavender—with no sense of extendedness.

**Moves.** The imperative "Try to imagine" puts the reader inside the test. The
argument contrasts a case where the test succeeds (integration) with one where it fails
(space), so the criterion is shown, not asserted. Short pivot sentence ("The same cannot
be done for space or time.") between two long ones.

## 4. Verdict, then a test the reader can run

*FAQ: Is IIT an emergentist theory of consciousness?*

> Is IIT an emergentist theory? More precisely, does IIT claim that consciousness
> "emerges" from a neural or physical basis? IIT's unequivocal answer is no—neither in a
> "strong" nor "weak" sense.
>
> […]
>
> How can we be sure that a relationship is of identity and not emergence? A simple test
> is to ask whether you can have the base without the emergent property. Can you have
> cars without a traffic jam? Yes. Can you have birds without a flock of them? Yes. Can
> you have a Φ-structure without an experience? No. In IIT, these are one and the same
> "thing," merely expressed in phenomenal terms vs. physical (causal) terms.

**Moves.** The question is sharpened before it is answered, and the answer is one word
with a qualification. The test is a series of very short question–answer beats that end
on the IIT case. This is the one place where rapid rhetorical questions are native to
the register: a test the reader can run, not decoration.

## 5. Answering by senses of a word

*FAQ: So, is IIT physicalist?*

> It depends. If *physical* means that the particles and forces in the standard model of
> physics are the "most real" ontological substrate from which everything else emerges
> or to which everything else is reducible, then no, IIT is not physicalist. If
> *physical* implies one of Chalmers's categories of materialism, then, again, no, IIT
> does not conform to this. But if *physical* is understood in an operational sense—as
> the cause–effect power we can unfold by perturbing and observing—then yes, IIT is
> physicalist. And if *physical* is meant in an intrinsic sense—as required to account
> for experience—then, again, yes, IIT's intrinsic powers ontology can also be called a
> physicalist ontology.

**Moves.** A two-word opener, then parallel conditionals, each a full sentence with its
own verdict. The anaphora ("If *physical*… If *physical*… But if… And if…") does the
work a bulleted list would do in chat, and keeps it an argument.

## 6. Conceding part of the worry

*FAQ: What counts as a "component" of experience?*

> Isn't this definition of components highly subjective? Yes, and it should be. In
> phenomenology, we talk about how things *feel* (how we experience them). And the
> feeling of what hangs together differs somewhat from person to person, and even for
> the same person over time. Through modulating your attention, you may even create
> components where there were none. […] For a percussionist, however, they are a single
> instrument called the *clave*.

**Moves.** Voices the reader's objection as a question, concedes it at once ("Yes, and
it should be."), and turns the concession into a point about phenomenology. The example
(two sticks that a percussionist hears as one instrument) is one sentence long.

## 7. Method argument with a short analogy

*FAQ: Why not build a theory of consciousness starting from empirical neuroscientific
research?*

> For a complete theory of consciousness, we should aim to know not just *that* a neural
> signature correlates with consciousness but *why*—why some neural areas and mechanisms
> comprise the NCCs and, just as importantly, why other neural areas and mechanisms do
> not. Only this way can we make strong inferences about consciousness being present *or
> absent* in cases where communication is impossible, or in cases where covert NCCs are
> involved.
>
> […] Likewise with NCCs: only if we have a theory of *why* they correlate with
> conscious states can we make strong inferences about both presence and absence.

**Moves.** Italics carry the contrast (*that* vs *why*, present *or absent*). The
analogy that sits between these paragraphs in the original (a child learning why the
refrigerator light goes off) is closed with "Likewise with NCCs:", which returns at once
to the claim. Analogies serve the argument and hand back to it.

## 8. Conceptual exposition at chapter pace

*Tononi & Boly 2025, "The quality of experience: space is extended"*

> Another, critical reason is that the experience of space, especially visual space, is
> partially penetrable through introspection. To characterize what it takes for space to
> feel extended, we should first abstract away from objects or local qualities: the sky
> feels extended whether or not it contains stars, and whether it is blue or black. We
> can then deploy spatial attention to begin dissecting its structure. We can easily
> 'spotlight' or 'pick out' any portion of experienced space—small, large, or anything in
> between, here, there, or anywhere in between. For lack of a better term, we call these
> phenomenal distinctions spots.

**Moves.** "We" as the investigators doing the introspection. A method stated as a
sequence of actions (abstract away, deploy attention, pick out), each with a concrete
anchor (the sky, blue or black). The technical term arrives last, once the reader has
the thing it names ("we call these phenomenal distinctions spots").

## 9. Technical definition in plain words

*FAQ: How do we get a TPM?*

> A transition probability matrix (TPM) is a matrix of numbers, each of which specifies
> the probability that the units will transition from one (input) state to another
> (output) state. […] A TPM forms the basis for assessing the cause–effect power and
> unfolding the full cause–effect structure of the substrate it represents.
>
> A TPM can be derived in two ways: through empirical manipulations or through modeling.
> In both situations it will only ever provide an approximation of the true transition
> probabilities: finite empirical experiments are always limited in their precision
> (assuming non-deterministic substrates), and models are only as good as the
> assumptions going into them.

**Moves.** Definition first, in words, with the acronym introduced once. Then its role
in the theory. Then a colon that introduces a two-way division, each branch justified in
the same sentence. Caveats go in parentheses so the main line stays clean.

## 10. What the exemplars have in common

- The first sentence answers, fixes a term, or restates the axiom at issue.
- Paragraphs are arguments of 3–6 sentences; lists are rare and reserved for genuinely
  parallel items.
- Long sentences carry the reasoning; short sentences deliver a verdict or a pivot
  ("Yes, and it should be."; "The same cannot be done for space or time.").
- Examples are homely, concrete, one or two sentences, and come after the claim.
- The reader is invited to check their own experience ("Try to imagine…", "Take visual
  experience.").
- Objections are voiced fairly, sometimes conceded in part, and answered from within the
  theory.
- Endings are substantive: a consequence, a distinction drawn, or a pointer to a paper.
